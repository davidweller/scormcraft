# AI Foundations & Responsible Use

*All Staff | Foundation level | ~60 minutes | Online, Self-Paced*

## Course Overview

This course starts from the question most staff actually arrive with: how is this going to help me do my job better tomorrow morning? Learners see role-relevant examples and complete a real task in the first ten minutes, then build the understanding and judgement to keep doing it well. It gives a clear, shared understanding of artificial intelligence and how it applies to everyday work in higher education and the public sector, moving past the hype to the practical skills needed to use AI tools confidently, capably, and responsibly. By the end, staff can identify where AI genuinely helps, spot the risks, decide when to use it, and write prompts that produce useful results.

## Target Audience

All staff in higher education institutions, colleges, and public sector organisations at foundation level. No prior experience with AI tools is required or assumed. This course is the recommended starting point for all staff before progressing to any other course in the WorkSmart-AI series.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, learners will be able to:

- **Distinguish** between key AI concepts (e.g., Generative vs. Predictive AI) and map them to common higher-education and public-sector use cases.
- **Identify** where AI can improve the work they already do, and where it cannot, so they can choose their first uses with confidence.
- **Apply** institutional principles of responsible AI use (including privacy, bias awareness, and compliance) to identify red flags in realistic workplace scenarios.
- **Evaluate** whether a task is appropriate for AI assistance using simple decision prompts.
- **Demonstrate** basic prompting skills (such as assigning role and context) to generate useful outputs while mitigating hallucinations and errors.

## Assessment Plan

Learners are assessed through a combination of interactive activities embedded in each module. Module 1 opens by answering "will AI take my job?", then moves to role-relevant examples and the Jargon Buster, a hands-on task in which learners run their first prompt and judge the result. A reflection follows, in which they name one task from their own week to hand over, and then the "Human or AI?" identification challenge tests critical awareness. Module 2 uses a Prediction Game to check understanding of how generative AI works. Module 3 uses a mode-comparison task, where learners run the same prompt in Standard and reasoning mode, with a follow-up question on matching mode to task. Module 4 includes the "Red Pen" scenario exercise, where learners identify privacy and ethics violations in a realistic case. Module 5 uses a set of gate-classification questions and a true or false check to assess application of the Decision Matrix. Module 6 requires learners to write their own prompt using the R-C-T framework and compare it against a model answer. Dialog card reviews in Modules 1 and 6 consolidate key terms. A brief knowledge check quiz is included at the end of the course to confirm achievement of all learning outcomes.

### The Question on Everyone's Mind

***"Will AI take my job?"***

It's the first thing most people want to know, so let's deal with it before anything else.

The AI tools in this course aren't autonomous agents that replace human judgement, empathy, or institutional knowledge. They don't have the compassion to steady an anxious student. They can't read the politics of a departmental meeting. They can't tell you whether a policy makes sense for *your* particular context.

What they can do is take on the repetitive, time-consuming parts of your work: drafting, summarising, reformatting, explaining. In a few minutes you'll run one yourself and see the shape of it. The tool will produce something useful, and you'll still be the one who decided it was needed, chose who it was for, and judged whether it was any good.

**This course isn't about replacing you. It's about reclaiming your time, and spending it on better work.**

There's a catch, and it's worth meeting early. These tools are fluent, and fluency is persuasive. Knowing when to believe them is the skill that makes everything else possible.

### What This Looks Like in Your Week

#### Start With Your Job, Not the Technology

Most people arrive at a course like this with one question: *how is this going to help me do my job better tomorrow morning?*

So let's answer that first, before any theory.

Below are the jobs that staff in higher education and the public sector most often hand to AI in their first fortnight of using it. Find the set closest to your role. You won't do all of these today. You need one.

#### If You Teach or Research

- Turn a dense reading into a one-page briefing your students will actually finish.
- Draft ten seminar discussion questions from a chapter, then keep the three that are any good.
- Rewrite a module description so a first-year applicant understands it.
- Get a second opinion on your own draft: "what have I assumed here that a reader won't know?"
- Produce a plain-English version of a marking rubric to hand out alongside the formal one.

#### If You Work in Professional Services or Administration

- Turn forty minutes of messy meeting notes into a clean set of actions and owners.
- Draft the email you've been putting off since Tuesday, then make it warmer or shorter in one follow-up.
- Reformat a bullet list into the table your report template wants.
- Summarise a long policy document into the five points your team actually needs.
- Write the first version of a process note, so you're editing rather than staring at a blank page.

#### If You Lead a Team or a Service

- Pressure-test a proposal before it goes to committee: "what are the three strongest objections to this?"
- Rewrite the same update three ways, for your team, for your director, and for external partners.
- Turn a strategy document into a one-page summary and a set of talking points.
- Structure a paper you know the content of but cannot find the shape for.
- Draft an agenda that respects everyone's time, from a list of things that need deciding.


Notice what isn't on these lists. Nothing here asks AI to make a decision, know a fact about your institution, or handle anything sensitive. Every example is drafting, summarising, reformatting, explaining, or challenging your own thinking. That isn't a coincidence, and by the end of this course you'll understand exactly why those tasks are the ones that work.

### You Are Already an AI User

#### Did You Know, You Already Use AI?

We often talk about Artificial Intelligence as if it's a futuristic wave coming for our jobs. The reality? You've probably used AI three times before your first meeting of the day.

**In your personal life:**

- Did you unlock your phone with FaceID this morning? That's computer vision.
- Did Netflix suggest something you actually wanted to watch? That's a recommendation engine.
- Did your email finish a sentence for you? That's predictive text.

**In your work:**

- Has your VLE ever flagged a student as "at risk" before you noticed a problem? That's predictive analytics.
- Has Turnitin compared a submission against millions of papers? That's pattern matching.
- Has your inbox sorted messages into "Focused" and "Other"? That's classification AI.

For years, we've used **Predictive AI** to help us classify data (for example, "Is this email spam? Yes or No").

What has changed recently - and why we are here today - is the rise of **Generative AI**. These tools do not just categorise data; they *create* it. They can write emails, draft lesson plans, summarise messy meeting notes, and generate first drafts of reports in seconds.

#### Activity: The "Human or AI?" Challenge

Read each passage below and decide whether it was written by a human or an AI.

Passage 1: "Thank you so much for the invitation. While I appreciate the opportunity to discuss the project, I currently have a conflict in my schedule that prevents me from attending. Please let me know if there are minutes I can review later."

**Q1 (Single choice)**

**Question:** Was Passage 1 written by a human or an AI?

- A) Human
- B) AI *(correct)*

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. Generative AI excels at corporate speak. It's polite, grammatically perfect, and efficient, often more polite than a busy human would bother to be.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. This one is AI. The giveaway isn't an error but the absence of one: no rush, no clipped phrasing, no personality. Frictionless politeness is a signal in itself.

Passage 2: "The paper argues that constructivist alignment is contingent upon the interplay between learner autonomy and rigid assessment structures, suggesting a bifurcation of traditional modalities."

**Q2 (Single choice)**

**Question:** Was Passage 2 written by a human or an AI?

- A) Human *(correct)*
- B) AI

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. Complexity and jargon aren't AI indicators. Humans, particularly academics, often write dense, specialised prose.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. This one is human. It's tempting to read difficulty as a machine signature, but the reverse is closer to the truth: AI tends to smooth prose out rather than knot it up. Do not assume that difficult text must be machine-generated.

Passage 3: "According to the University's 2024 Assessment Regulation Section 4.2, students are granted a 48-hour automated grace period for all coursework if submitted via the digital portal."

**Q3 (Single choice)**

**Question:** Was Passage 3 written by a human or an AI?

- A) Human
- B) AI *(correct)*

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct, and it's a complete fabrication. This regulation doesn't exist. The AI invented the clause number, the time period, and the submission route. This is called a hallucination, and it's the single most important concept in this course.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite, and the reason this one catches people is the point of the whole exercise. It reads as authoritative because it has a clause number, a specific time period, and a named route. None of it exists. The AI invented the lot. This is called a hallucination, and it's the single most important concept in this course.

#### Key Concepts

**Key Point:** Just because an AI sounds confident doesn't mean it's correct. Generative AI is a prediction engine, not a truth engine. It predicts the next likely word in a sentence based on patterns from billions of web pages. Sometimes, to complete a pattern convincingly, it will invent facts, citations, policies, or statistics.

This isn't a theoretical risk. In 2023, two New York lawyers submitted a court filing citing six cases - all invented by ChatGPT. The cases sounded real, with proper citations, but none existed. Both lawyers were sanctioned by the court. AI also regularly invents journal articles, complete with plausible author names, publication dates, and DOI numbers - none of which exist.

#### So Why Bother? The Time Equation

If AI can invent things this convincingly, why use it at all? Because when you point it at the right tasks and check its work, it hands back time you didn't have.

The time is only half the story. What matters is what the freed time buys, and that's where the real gain sits.

| Task | Without AI | With AI + Review | What the freed time buys |
|--------------------------|---------|-----------|--------------------------------------------|
| Drafting a professional email decline | 5-10 minutes | 1-2 minutes | The one email in your inbox that deserves a considered reply |
| Summarising a 20-page report | 45-60 minutes | 10-15 minutes | Time to interrogate the argument, not just extract the contents |
| Explaining a complex concept simply | 15-20 minutes | 3-5 minutes | Two explanations instead of one, so you can use the better |
| Generating 10 discussion questions | 20-30 minutes | 5-8 minutes | Room to shape the three questions worth actually asking |

Read the last column again. The point isn't that you produce more. It's that the thinking part of the job gets the hour the formatting part used to take.

The key is knowing *when* to use AI, *how* to use it well, and *what* to check afterwards. That's exactly what this course will teach you.

**Front:** What's Generative AI?
**Back:** A type of AI that creates new content (text, images, code) by predicting what should come next, based on patterns learned from billions of examples. Examples include ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude, and Gemini.

**Front:** What's Predictive AI?

## Module 2: What is AI?

### It's Not Magic, It's Maths

#### Introduction

Now that we know AI can fool us, we need to understand *how* it works. Not because you need to become a computer scientist, but because understanding the mechanism helps you predict when things will go wrong.

If you read the news, you might think AI is a sentient brain that "thinks," "reasons," and "understands" the world. It isn't. The AI tools we use today - ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude, Gemini - are sophisticated prediction engines. They do not *know* the answer; they *calculate* the most likely answer.

This distinction matters. A colleague who knows the answer will tell you if they're uncertain. A prediction engine will confidently give you whatever sounds most plausible - even if it's completely wrong.

To understand this properly, we need to separate the "Old AI" that has been in our tools for years from the "New AI" that arrived recently.

#### Two Types of AI

**Predictive AI (the "Librarian")** is what you've used for years. It looks at existing data and sorts it into categories. Examples in higher education include email spam filters, VLE early-warning systems that flag students at risk, library recommendation tools, and timetabling software that minimises room clashes. The output is a label, a score, or a sorted list - and accuracy is usually high because there's often a clear right answer.

**Generative AI (the "Creator")** is the newer technology, and the reason this course exists. It creates new content that resembles the content it was trained on. Tools include ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude, DALL-E, and GitHub Copilot. The output is new text, images, or code that didn't exist before. Accuracy is variable - because it's creating rather than retrieving, it can produce content that sounds perfect but is factually hollow.

**Key Point:** Predictive AI answers "Which box does this belong in?" Generative AI answers "What would a good answer look like?" That second question is why generative AI is both powerful and dangerous. It's optimising for plausibility, not truth.

#### The "Super Parrot" Analogy

Imagine a parrot with a perfect memory that has read every book in every university library, every Wikipedia article, every public webpage, every academic paper and news article. This parrot doesn't understand what it has read. It doesn't know what a "university" actually is, what "ethics" means, or whether something is true or false. But it has developed an extraordinarily sophisticated sense of which words tend to follow other words in different contexts.

When you ask the parrot a question, it doesn't search a database for the answer. Instead, it predicts - one word at a time - what a good response would look like, based on all the text it has seen.

#### Watching the Parrot Think

If you say to the parrot: *"The cat sat on the..."*

The parrot instantly calculates probabilities based on billions of sentences:

| Next word | Probability |
|-----------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| mat | 68% |
| floor | 15% |
| sofa | 8% |
| windowsill | 4% |
| refrigerator | 0.001% |

It picks "mat" (or samples from the top options) and moves on. Then it predicts the next word. And the next. Thousands of times per second, until it has built a complete response.

**This is why we call it "Generative." It's generating a statistically plausible sequence of words, not looking up facts in a database.**

### Why Hallucinations Happen

#### Hallucination Trigger Points

Remember the fake regulation from Module 1?

*"According to Regulation Section 4.2, students are granted a 48-hour automated grace period..."*

Why did the AI invent this? Because it wasn't trying to tell the truth. It was trying to complete a pattern.

In its training data:
- "Regulation" is often followed by "Section" and a number
- "Grace period" is often followed by a time like "48 hours" or "72 hours"
- Policy statements typically include specific references

The AI assembled these fragments into a sentence that *sounds* authoritative but has no factual basis.

AI is most likely to hallucinate when your request involves:

| Trigger | Example | Why it fails |
|---------------|---------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| Specific facts | "What's the phone number for Student Services?" | It may not have this data, but phone numbers follow predictable patterns |
| Recent events | "Summarise yesterday's Senate meeting" | Its training data has a cutoff date; it cannot know recent events |
| Niche or local information | "What are the opening hours of the main campus library?" | Specific institutional details may be absent or outdated |
| Citations and references | "Give me three academic sources on cognitive load theory" | It knows what citations *look like* but may invent plausible-sounding ones |
| Numbers and statistics | "What percentage of students use AI for assignments?" | It will generate a number that sounds reasonable, not one that's verified |

**Key Point:** The more specific, recent, or local your question, the higher the hallucination risk. General knowledge questions are relatively safe. Specific institutional questions are high-risk unless you provide the source material.

So, if the Parrot is just predicting words and might make things up, can you rely on it at work? Yes, once you're working with it properly: on the protected version (Module 3, Part 1), in the right mode for the task (Module 3, Part 2), and with the habits that keep one job from contaminating the next.

#### Activity: The Prediction Game

**Q1 (Single choice)**

**Question:** What word is a generative AI most likely to predict to complete the sentence "To be or not to..."?

- A) die
- B) be *(correct)*
- C) live
- D) think

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. In the training data, "be" follows "not to" in this context almost every time. The AI is extremely confident here because the pattern is unambiguous.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. The answer is "be". Each of the other options makes sense as a continuation, which is exactly why they're tempting, but the AI isn't reaching for sense. It's reaching for the most frequent pattern, and this particular sequence is one of the most repeated in the English language.

**Q2 (Single choice)**

**Question:** You ask an AI to complete: "According to my university's 2024 policy on generative AI..." What's the most likely outcome?

- A) The AI says "I don't know"
- B) The AI retrieves the exact policy from a database
- C) The AI generates something plausible but potentially invented *(correct)*
- D) The AI refuses to answer

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. The AI knows what a policy sounds like, but may not have your specific, current policy. Rather than saying "I don't know", it generates what a policy looks like, complete with invented clause numbers and confident phrasing.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. The answer is C, and the reasoning matters more than the answer. Options A and D assume the model can tell the difference between knowing something and not knowing it, which it cannot. Option B assumes it's looking something up, which it isn't. It's completing a pattern, and a policy-shaped gap gets filled with policy-shaped text whether or not the policy exists.

#### AI Strengths and Weaknesses

AI is strong at:
- Pattern-based tasks (drafting emails, reformatting text, explaining concepts simply)
- Creative ideation (generating options, brainstorming)
- Summarisation (condensing long documents when you provide the source)
- Translation and tone adjustment

AI is weak at:
- Factual accuracy (anything requiring verified or specific information)
- Maths and calculations (it predicts digits rather than calculating them)
- Institutional knowledge (your specific policies, procedures, and context)
- Judgement calls (decisions requiring empathy, ethics, or contextual understanding)

#### Summary

Generative AI is a sophisticated prediction engine - the "Super Parrot" - that generates statistically plausible text rather than retrieving facts. Hallucinations happen because the AI is completing patterns, not checking truth. The more specific, recent, or local your question, the higher the risk. You now know enough to predict where it will let you down, which is exactly the knowledge that lets you use it with confidence. In the next module, we'll set you up to work with the tool properly.

## Module 3: Your First AI Wins with Copilot

### Introduction

You've run one prompt and you understand why the answer came out the way it did. That combination, a win plus an explanation, is the foundation. Now we turn it into a working practice.

This module covers the three things that separate people who get consistent value from AI from people who try it twice and give up: working on the right version of the tool, choosing the right mode for the job, and keeping conversations clean.

For this course, we'll use **Microsoft Copilot**, as your institution provides access to a protected version. However, the principles we cover apply to any generative AI tool - ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or institutional chatbots. The interfaces differ, but the habits you need to develop are the same.

### Using the Right Version

There are two versions of most AI tools:

| Version | Your data | Who can see it |
|----------------|-----------------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Public/Free | May be stored, analysed, and used to train future AI models | Potentially anyone |
| Protected/Enterprise | Stays within your organisation's security boundary | Only you (and your IT team's logs) |

If you use a public version and paste in student names, grades, research data, or sensitive meeting notes, that information could end up anywhere. This isn't hypothetical - there have been documented cases of training data appearing in other users' outputs.

**Key Point:** Your institution has provided a protected version specifically to avoid this risk. But you must verify you are using it every time you open the tool.

#### The Green Shield Check

Before you type a single character of work-related content:

1. Open Copilot (or your institution's AI tool)
2. Look for a security indicator - typically in the top-right corner or near your profile
3. **Do you see a "Protected" badge, shield icon, or enterprise indicator?**

| What you see | What it means | What to do |
|-----------------------|----------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Green shield, "Protected", or Enterprise badge** | You're logged in with your work account. Data stays within your organisation. | Proceed with appropriate caution (still follow the 'Red Lines' - see Module 4) |
| **No indicator, "Free", or a personal account shown** | You may be using the public web version | **Stop.** Log out and sign in with your institutional credentials |
| **Unsure** | The interface may have changed | Check with your IT/Digital team before inputting any work data |

**Make this check a habit.** Every time you open the tool. Every time you switch devices. It takes two seconds and prevents serious data breaches.

**Key Insight:** Think about the devices and accounts you currently use for AI tools. Do you know whether you're using a protected version? What would you need to check or change?

#### What If Your Institution Uses a Different Tool?

The same principle applies regardless of which AI tool you use:

- **ChatGPT:** Look for "ChatGPT Enterprise" or "ChatGPT Team" indicator
- **Claude:** Check for organisation name in the interface
- **Google Gemini:** Verify you're in your Workspace account, not personal Google
- **Institutional chatbot:** These are typically protected by design, but confirm with IT

**The rule is universal: verify your protected status before inputting work data.**

### Modes and Habits

#### Standard Mode (Fast Response)

**What it's:** The default mode. The AI responds quickly using its standard prediction process.

**Speed:** Near-instant (starts generating within 1-2 seconds)

**Best for:**
- Drafting emails and routine correspondence
- Summarising text you've provided
- Explaining concepts in simpler terms
- Brainstorming ideas and options
- Reformatting or restructuring content

**Characteristics:** Good for volume, may be more superficial on complex problems.

#### "Think Deeper" / Reasoning Mode (Slower, More Thorough)

**What it's:** The AI takes longer to "reason" through the problem before responding. It may show its working or thinking process.

**Speed:** Slower (you may see a "Thinking..." animation for 10-30 seconds)

**Best for:**
- Complex analysis or multi-step problems
- Comparing options with trade-offs
- Planning or structuring a large piece of work
- Tasks requiring logical reasoning
- When your first attempt got a superficial answer

**Characteristics:** More thorough, but uses more computing resources (may have daily limits).

#### Activity: Same Prompt, Two Modes

**Key Insight:** Run this prompt in Standard mode: "We are considering moving our team's weekly update from a meeting to a written summary. What are the trade-offs?"

Now start a new chat, switch to Think Deeper or your tool's reasoning mode, and run the identical prompt.

Compare the two answers. The Standard version usually gives you a tidy list. The reasoning version tends to weigh the options against each other, surface the conditions under which each is right, and name what you would need to know to decide. Same prompt, different depth. Knowing which one a task deserves saves you both time and disappointment.

**Q1 (Single choice)**

**Question:** You need to compare three suppliers against cost, timescale, and accessibility requirements, and recommend one. Which mode fits best?

- A) Standard mode - it's faster and the answer will be similar
- B) Reasoning mode *(correct)*
- C) Neither - this task is unsuitable for AI

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. This is a multi-factor comparison ending in a recommendation, which is what reasoning mode is for. Note that the final recommendation is still yours; the tool is structuring the comparison, not making the decision.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. If you chose Standard mode, you'll get a serviceable summary of each supplier, but it tends to skate over the trade-offs between them, which is the whole job here. If you chose neither, you've been slightly too cautious: structuring a comparison is a good use of AI. Making the decision isn't, and that part stays with you.

#### The Fresh Start Habit

When you chat with an AI, it remembers everything in that conversation. This can cause problems when you switch topics - the AI may weirdly blend previous context into a new task, or errors from earlier may compound. The rule is simple: **New Task = New Chat.**

When you're finished with one task and starting something different, look for a "New Chat", "New Conversation", or "+" button and click it to clear the AI's memory of your previous conversation. If you're genuinely continuing the same task, stay in the same chat. If you're switching topics, start new.

#### Your First Session Checklist

**Key Point:** Before every AI session:

1. **Check protected status** - verify you see the enterprise/protected indicator.
2. **Start a new chat** if starting a new task.
3. **Choose your mode** - Standard for quick tasks, Reasoning for complex analysis.
4. **Be specific in your prompt** - clear input leads to better output.
5. **Review critically** - remember the parrot sounds confident but may be wrong.

#### Summary

You now know how to get onto the protected version, match the mode to the task, and keep your conversations clean. Those three habits are what make AI reliable rather than occasionally impressive. In the next module, we'll cover the "Red Lines", the small number of things you must never do, and the professional judgement that sits around them.

## Module 4: Confidence and Professional Judgement

### Introduction

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QZlfeafQo1c&t=2s](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QZlfeafQo1c&t=2s)

In Module 2, we learned that Generative AI is like a "Super-Read Parrot" that predicts words based on patterns. Here's the catch: Parrots repeat what they hear.

If you type confidential information into a public AI tool (like the free version of ChatGPT or generic online chatbots), you're effectively sending that data to an external computer. That data might be used to train the next version of the model.

This brings us to the part of AI literacy that professionals rely on most: knowing exactly where the "Red Lines" are. There are fewer of them than people expect. Knowing them precisely is what lets you use everything on the other side of them without hesitating.

### The Privacy Red Line

#### The Golden Rule

**Key Point:** If you would not email it to a stranger, do not paste it into AI.

Even with enterprise protection, AI interactions are logged, may be reviewed by IT administrators, and could potentially be subject to legal discovery. Treat the AI like a very efficient but indiscreet colleague.

#### What is Personally Identifiable Information (PII)?

PII is any data that could identify a specific individual, either directly or in combination with other information.

**Direct identifiers (never input these):**

| Category | Examples |
|-------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Names | Student names, staff names, external contacts |
| ID numbers | Student IDs, staff numbers, National Insurance numbers |
| Contact details | Email addresses, phone numbers, home addresses |
| Financial data | Bank details, salary information, bursary amounts |
| Authentication | Passwords, security answers, access codes |

**Indirect identifiers (use caution):**

These might not identify someone alone, but combined with other information could do so:

| Category | Examples | Risk |
|----------------------|----------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Course + Year + Characteristic | "The mature student in Level 2 Nursing who..." | May identify individual in small cohorts |
| Role + Department + Issue | "The technician in Chemistry who raised a grievance..." | Could narrow to one person |
| Unique circumstances | "The student whose visa was rejected in October..." | Context may make identification possible |

**Key Point:** Under UK GDPR, some data requires extra protection. Never input health and medical information, race and ethnicity data, religious beliefs, sexual orientation, political opinions, trade union membership, biometric data, or criminal records into AI tools - even protected ones - without explicit guidance from your Data Protection Officer.

#### The Anonymisation Option

Sometimes you need AI help with a task that involves real situations. The solution: **anonymise before you paste.**

| Instead of... | Use... |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| "Draft feedback for John Smith (ID: 12345678)" | "Draft feedback for Student A" |
| "Summarise the extenuating circumstances for Sarah in Level 3 Biology" | "Summarise the following extenuating circumstances case: [paste details with names removed]" |
| "Write a reference for Dr James Wilson in Chemistry" | "Write a reference for a Senior Lecturer in a science department with the following achievements: [list achievements]" |

**The rule:** Remove names, IDs, and identifying details *before* the information enters the AI. You can add them back to the output afterwards.

### The Bias and Accountability Red Lines

#### The Bias Red Line

Remember: AI learned to write by reading the internet. The internet contains humanity's knowledge - and humanity's biases.

AI models absorb and reflect the stereotypes present in their training data. This isn't a flaw that can be "fixed" - it's inherent to how the technology works.

**Bias in Practice: HE Examples**

**Gender bias:**

Ask AI to write a profile of a "professor" and it will often default to male pronouns and stereotypically male interests. Ask for a "nurse" or "teaching assistant" and watch the pronouns shift.

*Your responsibility:* Review AI-generated content for gendered assumptions, especially in job descriptions, case studies, and example scenarios.

**Cultural bias:**

AI training data over-represents Western, English-language sources. This affects:

| Area | How bias appears |
|-----------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Names in examples | Default to Anglo-American names (John, Sarah) rather than diverse names |
| Cultural references | Examples assume Western contexts (Christmas breaks, not Eid or Diwali) |
| Food and lifestyle | "Healthy breakfast" defaults to oatmeal and eggs, not congee, dosa, or fuul |
| Academic citations | May over-represent Western scholarship, under-represent Global South researchers |

*Your responsibility:* Actively diversify AI-generated examples. Do not accept the defaults as neutral.

**Disability bias:**

AI may reproduce ableist assumptions or language. Medical-model framing ("suffering from," "confined to a wheelchair") may appear instead of social-model language ("disabled by barriers," "wheelchair user").

*Your responsibility:* Check AI-generated content about disability for appropriate, respectful language aligned with your institution's inclusive practice guidelines.

#### The "Reasonable Person" Test

Before using AI-generated content, ask yourself:

*"If a student, colleague, or external stakeholder from any background read this, would they feel respected and included?"*

If there's any doubt, edit before publishing.

#### The Accountability Red Line

If you submit, send, or publish AI-generated content, you're responsible for it. You cannot say "the AI wrote it," "I didn't notice the error," or "the tool made a biased assumption." If your name is on it, you own it.

If AI drafts an email with an incorrect deadline, you sent an email with an incorrect deadline. If AI generates a reading list with fabricated citations, you recommended readings that do not exist. If AI writes student feedback with a gendered assumption, you gave biased feedback to a student. The AI is a tool. You're the professional.

### The "Red Pen" Exercise

#### Scenario: Alex's Shortcut

Alex is a Department Administrator. It's Friday afternoon, and they're overwhelmed. A sensitive Student Support Committee meeting just finished, and Alex needs to write up the outcomes and notify the student involved. Alex decides to save time by pasting their rough notes into the AI chatbot.

Alex's prompt: "Please summarise these notes from the support meeting and draft an email to the student: Meeting: Student Support Committee, 15 Nov 2024. Present: Dr Harrison (Chair), Alex (Admin), Student Wellbeing Officer. Case: John Smith (ID: 2345678) - Extenuating circumstances application. Reason: Severe anxiety and depression following family bereavement. Evidence: GP letter confirming diagnosis. Request: Extension for all Semester 1 assessments. Committee decision: Application REJECTED - insufficient evidence of impact on specific assessments. Student advised to resubmit with more detail. Action: Alex to notify student of outcome."

**Q1 (Single choice)**

**Question:** How many distinct problems can you identify in Alex's prompt?

- A) One - sharing a student name
- B) Two - sharing name and health information
- C) Three - name, health information, and confidential committee outcomes
- D) Four *(correct)*

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. There are four distinct red flags, listed below. Spotting all four is harder than it looks, because the fourth isn't about data at all.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite, and how far you got is worth noting. Most people find the name, and many find the health information. Fewer spot that the committee's decision is itself confidential. Few people count the fourth, because it isn't a data problem: delegating a sensitive rejection to a machine is a judgement failure, not a privacy one. All four are set out below.

**Key Point:** The four red flags:

- **Red Flag 1 - Student name and ID:** sharing PII with an AI system creates an unnecessary record linking a named individual to sensitive circumstances. Fix: use "Student A" or "The Applicant".
- **Red Flag 2 - Health information:** mental health diagnosis is Special Category Data under UK GDPR. Fix: generalise to "health-related circumstances" without diagnostic detail.
- **Red Flag 3 - Bereavement details:** unnecessary personal context. The AI only needs to know there was an EC application and the outcome.
- **Red Flag 4 - Delegating a sensitive rejection to AI:** a student receiving a rejection while experiencing mental health difficulties needs human empathy, not machine-generated correspondence.

#### The Corrected Approach

If Alex genuinely needs help, a responsible version would be: "Draft a professional email template for notifying a student that their extenuating circumstances application was unsuccessful, with an invitation to resubmit with additional information. The tone should be supportive and constructive." Alex then receives a generic template, personalises it with appropriate care and empathy, reviews for tone given this student's specific situation, and sends it as their own professional communication. Time saved: still significant. Risk: eliminated.

#### The Three Principles of Responsible Use

**1. Transparency**

If you use AI to create content for students or colleagues, acknowledge it.

Examples:
- *"Drafted with AI assistance, reviewed and edited by [Name]"*
- *"Initial structure generated using Copilot, content verified against [source]"*

This isn't about shame - it's about honesty. Many institutions are developing norms around AI acknowledgement. Check your local guidance.

**2. Accountability**

You're responsible for the output. Always.

Treat verification as part of the craft rather than a box to tick. A good editor reads a draft looking for what the writer missed; do the same here, and you'll produce better work than either you or the tool would alone.

This means:
- Verify facts before sharing
- Check for bias before publishing
- Ensure tone is appropriate for the recipient
- Confirm policy references are accurate

**3. Data Minimisation**

Only give the AI what it needs. Nothing more.

Ask yourself: *"What's the minimum context required for this task?"*

| Task | Minimum context | Unnecessary additions |
|------------------------|----------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Draft a meeting agenda | Topic list, time constraints | Who said what in previous meetings |
| Explain a concept simply | The concept, target audience | Which specific student asked |
| Summarise a document | The document content | Why you need the summary, who it's for |

#### Summary

You now understand the three Red Lines: privacy (never input PII or special category data), bias (review all outputs for stereotypes and assumptions), and accountability (you own everything you submit or publish). The test questions to ask are: "Would I email this to a stranger?", "Would everyone feel respected reading this?", and "Am I prepared to defend this as my work?" These are the same standards you already apply to your own work; you're extending them to a new tool, not learning a new profession. In the next module, we'll give you a practical framework for deciding, task by task, whether AI is the right tool for the job.

#### Your Institution's Specific Guidance

The principles above are universal. Your institution may have additional specific requirements.

**Check your local guidance on:**
- Approved AI tools (which tools have institutional agreements?)
- Data classification (what types of data can be processed?)
- Acknowledgement requirements (when must AI use be disclosed?)
- Research data (specific rules for research contexts?)
- Student-facing use (rules about AI in teaching and assessment?)

If you're unsure whether a specific use is appropriate, ask before proceeding.

## Module 5: The Decision Matrix - To Use or Not to Use?

### The Three Gates

#### Introduction: Two Different Questions

You now understand the Red Lines - what you must never input into AI. Working well with AI means answering two separate questions, and most tasks clear both in a couple of seconds:

1. **Can I use AI for this?** (Is it safe and permitted?)
2. **Should I use AI for this?** (Is it effective and appropriate?)

Something can be *permitted* but still be a *bad idea*. For example:
- Asking AI to calculate exam grade boundaries isn't a privacy breach, but it's a bad idea because AI is unreliable at maths
- Asking AI to draft a bereavement condolence message isn't illegal, but it may be inappropriate because such messages need genuine human empathy

**Key Insight:** Think of the Decision Matrix as a traffic light system. Before using AI for any task, run it through three "gates" in order. You must pass all three to proceed safely.

#### Gate 1: The Privacy Gate (Can I?)

Stop if the task contains:
- Personal Identifiable Information (student names, staff names, ID numbers, email addresses, phone numbers)
- Special Category Data (health information, disability details, religious observance, political views)
- Confidential institutional data (unpublished research, financial records, strategic plans)
- Others' intellectual property
- Information that could indirectly identify individuals

**If YES to any → 🛑 STOP.** Your options:
1. **Anonymise first:** Remove all identifying information, then reassess
2. **Do it manually:** Some tasks simply should not involve AI
3. **Seek guidance:** Ask your Data Protection Officer or line manager if genuinely unsure

**If NO to all → Proceed to Gate 2**

#### Gate 2: The Accuracy Gate (Should I?)

Proceed with caution if:
- 100% accuracy is critical (grade calculations, regulatory compliance, legal statements)
- This is a high-stakes decision affecting someone's life or career (scholarship decisions, hiring recommendations, disciplinary outcomes)
- The task requires current or local information (today's timetable, recent policy changes)
- The task involves precise numbers or calculations (budget totals, statistical analysis)
- You would struggle to verify the output

**If YES to any → ⚠️ CAUTION.** Use AI for a first draft but verify every fact and calculation independently. Use appropriate tools for calculations (Excel). Seek expert review if you cannot verify the output yourself.

**If NO to all → Proceed to Gate 3**

#### Gate 3: The Suitability Gate (Is AI the right tool?)

Green light if the task involves: drafting and reformulating (email drafts, rewriting for different audiences), summarising content you provide (condensing reports, extracting key points), explaining and simplifying (making complex concepts accessible), ideation and brainstorming (generating options, suggesting approaches), structuring and organising (outlining documents, creating frameworks), reformatting (converting lists to tables, standardising layouts), or getting feedback on your own drafts.

**If the task fits → ✅ GO** - with review. Remember to verify any facts, check for bias, and edit to match your voice and the specific context.

**If the task doesn't fit:** consider whether the "old way" is faster, more reliable, or more appropriate.

#### The Traffic Light System

**🛑 Red (Can I?)**

*The question: "What data am I about to input?"*

| Check | Examples |
|-------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| Contains Personal Identifiable Information (PII)? | Student names, staff names, ID numbers, email addresses, phone numbers |
| Contains Special Category Data? | Health information, disability details, religious observance, political views |
| Contains confidential institutional data? | Unpublished research, financial records, strategic plans, security information |
| Contains others' intellectual property? | Copyrighted textbooks, colleagues' unpublished work, proprietary materials |
| Could identify individuals indirectly? | Small cohort + specific characteristic, unique circumstances |

**If YES to any → 🛑 STOP**

Your options:
1. **Anonymise first:** Remove all identifying information, then reassess
2. **Do it manually:** Some tasks simply should not involve AI
3. **Seek guidance:** Ask your Data Protection Officer or line manager if genuinely unsure

**If NO to all → Proceed to Gate 2**

**⚠️ Yellow (Should I?)**

*The question: "What happens if the AI gets this wrong?"*

| Check | Examples | Why it's risky |
|----------------------------|-------------------------------------|------------------------|
| Is 100% accuracy critical? | Grade calculations, regulatory compliance, legal statements | AI makes confident errors |
| Is this a high-stakes decision affecting someone's life or career? | Scholarship decisions, hiring recommendations, disciplinary outcomes | Requires human judgement |
| Does this require current or local information? | Today's timetable, your department's specific process, recent policy changes | AI training data has a cutoff; may not know your context |
| Does this involve precise numbers or calculations? | Budget totals, statistical analysis, date calculations | AI predicts digits, doesn't calculate |
| Would I struggle to verify the output? | Code I cannot read, legal advice I cannot assess, technical claims outside my expertise | Cannot catch errors you cannot recognise |

**If YES to any → ⚠️ CAUTION**

Your options:
1. **Human-in-the-loop:** Use AI for a first draft, but verify every fact and calculation independently
2. **Use appropriate tools:** For calculations, use Excel. For current information, check primary sources.
3. **Seek expert review:** If you cannot verify the output yourself, have someone who can review it before use
4. **Reduce the stakes:** Use AI for brainstorming or structure, but write the final high-stakes content yourself

**If NO to all → Proceed to Gate 3**

**✅ Green (Is AI the right tool?)**

*The question: "Is this task a good fit for AI's strengths?"*

| AI Strength | Examples | Why AI helps |
|------------------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Drafting and reformulating | Email drafts, letter templates, rewriting for different audiences | Pattern-based text generation is AI's core capability |
| Summarising provided content | Condensing reports, extracting key points from documents you supply | Compression of provided information (not retrieval) |
| Explaining and simplifying | Making complex concepts accessible, creating analogies | Translating between registers and complexity levels |
| Ideation and brainstorming | Generating options, suggesting approaches, listing possibilities | Quantity of ideas without commitment to any |
| Structuring and organising | Outlining documents, creating frameworks, suggesting headings | Pattern recognition for structure |
| Reformatting and transforming | Converting lists to tables, changing formats, standardising layouts | Mechanical transformation tasks |
| Feedback on your drafts | "How could I make this clearer?", "What have I missed?" | Fresh perspective on your existing work |

**If your task fits these categories → ✅ GO**

Remember:
- Review the output before using it
- Verify any facts, citations, or statistics
- Check for bias and inappropriate assumptions
- Edit to match your voice and the specific context

**If your task doesn't fit → Consider alternatives:**
- Quick personal messages → Just write them
- Simple factual lookups → Search your institution's website or documentation
- Emotional or sensitive communications → Human authorship matters
- Tasks requiring real-time information → Check primary sources directly

### Applying the Matrix

#### Activity: Apply the Matrix

**Q1 (Single choice)**

**Question:** "Generate five possible titles for a workshop on digital accessibility." Which gate applies?

- A) Gate 1: Stop
- B) Gate 2: Caution
- C) Gate 3: Go *(correct)*

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. This is pure ideation with no personal data and no accuracy requirements. You'll choose the best title yourself. This is exactly what AI does well.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. Run it through the gates in order. There's no personal data, so Gate 1 is clear. Nothing here has to be factually right, because you're generating options you'll judge yourself, so Gate 2 is clear. That leaves Gate 3, and ideation is squarely in AI's strengths. Green light.

**Q2 (Single choice)**

**Question:** "Calculate the final module marks for my 45 students based on their component grades and weightings." Which gate applies?

- A) Gate 1: Stop *(correct)*
- B) Gate 2: Caution only
- C) Gate 3: Go

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. Two problems, and the first one stops you. Privacy: student grades are personal data, and you would need to input names or IDs alongside the marks. Accuracy: AI is unreliable at arithmetic. Use a spreadsheet with verified formulas instead.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. If you chose Gate 2, you spotted the accuracy problem, which is real, but you got there one gate too late: the marks are attached to identifiable students, so Gate 1 stops this before accuracy is even considered. That ordering is the point of the matrix. Use a spreadsheet with verified formulas instead.

**Q3 (Single choice)**

**Question:** "Reformat my bullet-point lecture notes into a structured handout with headings and subheadings." Which gate applies?

- A) Gate 1: Stop
- B) Gate 2: Caution
- C) Gate 3: Go *(correct)*

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. This is reformatting your own content, an ideal use case. No personal data, assuming the notes do not contain student information, low accuracy stakes, and it plays to AI's strengths.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. It's worth asking what made you hesitate. The word "lecture" doesn't make something risky, and reformatting doesn't introduce new facts that could be wrong. The one thing worth checking is whether your notes happen to contain student information, and if they do not, this is a green light.

**Q4 (True or false)**

**Statement:** If a task passes Gate 1 (privacy), you can go ahead and use AI without further checks.

**Answer:** False

**Feedback (if correct):** Correct. You must pass all three gates. A task may be privacy-safe but still be a poor idea if accuracy is critical, verification is difficult, or the task doesn't suit AI's actual strengths.

**Feedback (if incorrect):** Not quite. Privacy is the first gate, not the only one. Calculating exam grade boundaries from anonymous data breaches nobody's privacy and is still a bad idea, because AI predicts digits rather than calculating them. "Can I?" and "should I?" are different questions.

#### Making It Automatic

The goal is for this decision process to become instinctive. In weeks 1-2, deliberately pause before each AI use and mentally run through the three gates. By weeks 3-4, you'll notice when you're about to skip the check. After week 5, the assessment becomes automatic - you'll feel uncomfortable when something doesn't pass the gates.

When in doubt: default to caution (do it manually or seek guidance), ask a colleague, contact your institution's AI lead or Data Protection Officer, or check your institutional policy.

#### Summary

The Decision Matrix is a three-gate traffic light system: Gate 1 (Privacy) - does this involve personal or confidential data? Gate 2 (Accuracy) - what happens if the AI is wrong? Gate 3 (Suitability) - is this drafting, summarising, explaining, or brainstorming? Only proceed when all three gates are clear. Used properly the framework does two jobs: it prevents the mistakes, and it gets you to a confident yes far more often than a no, because you no longer have to hesitate over every task.

## Module 6: Prompting Basics - The R-C-T Framework

### Why Prompts Matter

#### Garbage In, Garbage Out

Remember the parrot analogy from Module 2? If you ask a parrot to "Say something," it might squawk random noise. If you ask it to "Recite the first line of Hamlet," it knows exactly which words to predict.

Ask an AI for "something about deadlines" and you'll get something vague about deadlines. When the output disappoints, that's usually not the AI's fault. It's a prompting fault, and it's the easiest of all these problems to fix.

**Key Point:** The quality of the AI's output is directly tied to the specific details in your input.

This module teaches you a simple framework - **R-C-T** - that transforms mediocre prompts into effective ones.

#### The R-C-T Framework

You do not need to be a software engineer to get good results. You just need a simple structure. The R-C-T Framework has three components: Role, Context, Task.

| Letter | Element | What it does |
|--------------|----------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|
| R | Role | Tells the AI *who* to be |
| C | Context | Tells the AI *what* it needs to know |
| T | Task | Tells the AI *what* to produce |

Think of it as briefing a new colleague. You wouldn't just say "write something about deadlines." You would explain who they're writing as, what the situation is, and exactly what you need from them.

#### R - Role: Who Is the AI Today?

The Role sets the AI's perspective, expertise, and voice.

Without a role, the AI defaults to "generic helpful assistant" - which produces generic, undifferentiated output. Assigning a role activates specific patterns from the AI's training:

- "Act as a strict academic editor" → Critical, precise, focused on errors
- "Act as a supportive student advisor" → Warm, encouraging, solution-focused
- "Act as a concise executive" → Brief, direct, action-oriented

**Role Examples:**

| Your task | Effective role |
|------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| Proofreading an assessment brief | "Act as a meticulous academic editor who catches ambiguity and unclear instructions" |
| Drafting a student-facing email | "Act as a friendly but professional Student Services advisor" |
| Simplifying a complex policy | "Act as a plain-English specialist who makes bureaucratic text accessible" |
| Generating discussion questions | "Act as an experienced seminar leader in [subject area]" |
| Writing a reference letter | "Act as a supportive academic referee who highlights strengths professionally" |
| Creating a meeting agenda | "Act as an efficient administrator who values people's time" |

#### C - Context: What Does the AI Need to Know?

The Context provides the background information the AI cannot guess.

Remember: AI is a prediction engine, not a mind reader. It doesn't know:
- Who your audience is
- What has happened before
- What constraints you're working within
- What your institution's culture is like
- What outcome you actually need

If you do not provide this, the AI will guess - and guessing means generic.

**The Four Context Questions:**

| Question | What to include |
|------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| Who is this for? | The audience: students, colleagues, external partners, senior leadership |
| What's the situation? | Relevant background: what happened, what is happening, what is coming |
| What are the constraints? | Length limits, tone requirements, format needs, things to avoid |
| What does success look like? | The goal: to inform, persuade, reassure, instruct, celebrate |

**Weak context:** *"Write an email about assignment extensions."*

**Strong context:** *"The audience is first-year undergraduate students who are new to university and may be anxious about asking for help. The situation is that we've a new online system for requesting extensions, launching next week. The email should be reassuring (not bureaucratic), under 200 words, and must include the link to the new system."*

The weak version could produce anything. The strong version guides the AI toward exactly what you need.

#### T - Task: What Exactly Do You Want?

The Task specifies the output format and any structural requirements.

"Write something about X" invites the AI to choose the format. It might give you an essay when you wanted bullet points, a formal letter when you wanted a casual message, 500 words when you needed 100, or a list when you needed flowing prose. Being explicit about the *form* of the output saves editing time.

| Form item | Examples |
|---------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Format | "Write a bullet-point list" / "Create a table" / "Draft a three-paragraph email" |
| Length | "In exactly 100 words" / "No more than one page" / "A brief summary (2-3 sentences)" |
| Structure | "Use headings for each section" / "Start with the key action, then explain why" |
| Tone | "Keep the tone encouraging" / "Be direct and professional" |
| Inclusions | "Include a clear call to action" / "End with next steps" / "Mention the deadline" |
| Exclusions | "Do not include jargon" / "Avoid passive voice" / "Do not assume prior knowledge" |

### The R-C-T Framework in Practice

#### Worked Example: The Makeover

**Without R-C-T:** "Write an email about the library renovation." The AI will write a generic, boring email. It might invent dates. It might be too formal or too casual. It has no idea if the library is closing for a day or a year.

**With R-C-T:**

> [Role] Act as the Head Librarian.
> [Context] We are closing the main floor for painting next week (Monday-Wednesday). The quiet study rooms remain open. The audience is stressed students preparing for exams.
> [Task] Draft a short, reassuring email notifying them of the closure. Emphasise that study spaces are still available. Keep it under 200 words.

**Key Point:** The R-C-T prompt produces a specific, empathetic, and accurate draft that requires very little editing. The difference isn't the AI - it's the quality of the instruction.

#### Activity: Prompting Lab

**Key Insight:** You're an administrator with messy notes from a "Welcome Week" planning meeting. You need to turn them into a clear agenda for the next meeting. Write a prompt using the R-C-T framework. A strong answer would include: Role ("Act as an efficient Meeting Coordinator"), Context ("I have pasted rough notes below from our planning session. The team is overwhelmed, so we need clarity"), and Task ("Summarise these notes into a structured meeting agenda for next week. Use clear headings and bold the action items").

#### The Follow-Up (Iterating)

Even with R-C-T, the first result isn't always perfect. Do not give up. Treat the AI like an intern - if the intern brings you a draft that's too long, you wouldn't fire them. You would say: "Good start, but make it shorter." Common follow-up prompts include:

- "Make it more professional"
- "That's too long - summarise it in 3 bullet points"
- "Change the tone to be friendlier"
- "Format that as a table"

#### Flashcards: R-C-T Review

**Front:** What does the R in R-C-T stand for?
**Back:** Role - telling the AI who to pretend to be, which sets the perspective and expertise of the response.

**Front:** What does the C in R-C-T stand for?
**Back:** Context - giving the AI the background information it needs: the audience, the goal, and any constraints.

**Front:** What does the T in R-C-T stand for?
**Back:** Task - specifying exactly what you want, including the format (email, bullet list, table) and any length requirements.

#### Summary

The quality of your AI output is determined by the quality of your input. The R-C-T Framework gives you a simple, memorable structure: Role (who the AI should be), Context (the background information), and Task (exactly what you want). Iterate when needed - treat the first response as a draft, not a final answer.
